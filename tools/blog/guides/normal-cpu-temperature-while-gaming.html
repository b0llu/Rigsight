Your CPU says 83°C halfway through a match and you want to know if that's okay. The normal CPU temperature while gaming depends a lot on which chip you have, because AMD and Intel tune their processors to run up to very different limits. Here are the ranges, the makers' own numbers by model, and what to check when yours runs hot.

Normal CPU temperature while gaming on a desktop

On a desktop with a decent cooler, most CPUs sit somewhere between 60 and 85°C in a game. Games rarely load every core at once, so they run cooler than a stress test or a long video export.

Readings jump around much more than a graphics card's. A CPU can go from 55 to 80°C and back within a second when a game loads a new area. That's normal. Look at where it settles over a few minutes of play, not at single spikes.

Modern CPUs also boost their clock speed until they run into a limit on power or temperature. A chip that reaches the high 80s isn't always struggling. Some are built to use all the room they have.

The limits AMD and Intel give, by model

Every CPU has a maximum temperature in its spec, often called Tjmax or TJunction. At that point it slows itself down to protect the chip. There is no single number that fits every CPU. Here are the makers' figures for some common chips.

  • The AMD Ryzen 5 5600X is rated to 95°C. AMD has said up to 95°C is typical and by design at full load on this chip.
  • The AMD Ryzen 7 5800X, Ryzen 9 5900X and 5950X are rated to 90°C, which AMD called typical at full load.
  • The AMD Ryzen 7000 X series, such as the 7700X, is rated to 95°C.
  • The AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D is rated to 89°C, lower than the other Ryzen 7000 chips.
  • The AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D is rated to 95°C.
  • The Intel Core i9 14900K is rated to 100°C.
  • The Intel Core Ultra 9 285K is rated to 105°C.

For any other chip, search its name on AMD's or Intel's own site and look for "Max. Operating Temperature" or "TJUNCTION". Intel describes throttling as the processor lowering its clock speed when it goes above that figure.

Normal CPU temperature while gaming on Ryzen

Ryzen chips have a reputation for running hot, and AMD has been open that this is on purpose. Soon after the Ryzen 5000 series came out, AMD's technical marketing director said temperatures up to 90 or 95°C at full load were typical and by design, because a higher allowed temperature lets the chip boost for longer before it backs off.

In games, most Ryzen desktops with a reasonable cooler sit in the 60s to 80s. The Ryzen 7 7800X3D is the one to watch more closely. Its ceiling is 89°C, where other Ryzen 7000 chips have 95°C.

Normal CPU temperature while gaming with an AIO

An all-in-one liquid cooler moves heat to a radiator, which usually gives you lower peaks than a small tower cooler. Readings in the 60s and low 70s are common in games. The liquid takes time to warm up, so an AIO can look very cool for the first few minutes and then rise to a steady level.

If a CPU with an AIO hits its limit in games, the cooler usually isn't working properly. Intel's own checklist says to make sure the pump and radiator fans are plugged into the right motherboard headers. A pump on a fan header that slows it down at idle, or a pump that has failed, gives exactly this symptom.

Normal CPU temperature while gaming on a laptop

Laptop CPUs share a small cooler with the GPU, and readings in the 80s and low 90s are common in games. The laptop maker decides the power limits and how fast the fans spin, so a laptop that sits at 90°C may be doing exactly what it was tuned to do. Intel's support pages say as much. On a laptop the maker decides the power limits, so Intel points laptop owners to the maker.

The easy wins are the same as for any laptop. Play on a hard surface, keep the vents clean, and try the maker's quieter or balanced mode to see whether the temperature falls more than the frame rate.

How to check your CPU temperature

Windows' Task Manager shows your graphics card's temperature, but not your CPU's. You have a few other ways to see it.

  1. The BIOS or UEFI setup screen shows the CPU temperature, but only at rest, which tells you little about gaming.
  2. AMD's Ryzen Master and Intel's Extreme Tuning Utility both read their own chips.
  3. A sensor app such as HWiNFO reads almost every chip and can log readings to a file while you play.

Whichever you use, look at the highest reading during a session. The number you see after you alt-tab out can be lower than the peak was.

What to do if your CPU runs hot in games

Intel's support checklist for a CPU that runs hot is a good order for any brand.

  1. Make sure the cooler is rated for your CPU. Intel's rule is that the cooler's rated wattage should be at least the processor's base power. A small cooler that came with a 65 W chip won't keep up with a 125 W one.
  2. Check the cooler is mounted firmly and the protective plastic film was removed from its base. It's an easy thing to miss on a new build.
  3. Check the thermal paste. If the PC is several years old, or the cooler has been taken off and put back, fresh paste can help.
  4. Check that every fan spins, including the CPU cooler's own fan or the AIO's radiator fans.
  5. Check case airflow, so the cooler isn't breathing air the graphics card already heated.
  6. Look at the BIOS settings. Intel notes that some motherboards ship with default settings that run beyond Intel's guidelines, and suggests loading the BIOS defaults or asking the motherboard maker whether overclocking settings are turned on by default. Change BIOS settings only if you're comfortable there, and write down what you change.

A frame cap helps the CPU too, because fewer frames means less work per second. For the full list of fixes for the whole PC, see PC overheating while gaming. If you're comparing against the graphics card, normal GPU temperature while gaming has the GPU side.

How RigSight shows your CPU temperature

RigSight is a free, open-source PC monitor for Windows. It records your CPU temperature all day and keeps it as history, so you can see how hot it got during last night's game without having started a log first. Like most tools that read the CPU's own sensors, it needs admin rights for CPU temperature and power. The Temperatures page says so on the CPU card if they're missing.

The Temperatures page shows the CPU now, with today's lowest and highest under the gauge, and a history graph for any range up to the last 12 months. Each minute can be shown as its average, highest or lowest, so a short spike doesn't hide inside an average. The page also shows how warm the CPU is at rest next to its usual resting temperature, and charts the resting temperature over the last 30 days. A slow climb there points to dust or old paste.

For a week, a month or a custom range, its Against your usual table compares the CPU in the game you played most with your own usual in that game from the weeks before. That tells you whether 83°C is normal for your PC, which matters more than any range online. You can also turn on an alert under Notifications > Temperature alerts in Settings. The CPU limit starts at 85° and can be set from 60 to 100°.

Next, look up your exact CPU's maximum on AMD's or Intel's site, then play for half an hour and note where the temperature settles. If it stays 10 degrees or more under the limit, you're fine. If it sits at the limit, start with the cooler mount and the fans.
