You see two numbers in a benchmark, average FPS and 1% low. Most people look at the first one. The second one is the reason a game at 120 FPS can still feel choppy. Here is the 1% low FPS meaning in plain words, what a good gap to the average is, and what to do when yours is bad.

What 1% low FPS means

A game doesn't make frames at an even pace. Some frames take 7 ms, some take 9 ms, and now and then one takes 30 ms. Average FPS hides that. It adds all the frames up and divides by the time.

The 1% low looks only at the slowest frames. Take every frame from a stretch of play, sort them from fastest to slowest, and look at the slowest 1%. Turn that frame time into a frame rate, and you have the 1% low. A frame rate is 1,000 divided by the frame time in milliseconds, so these frame times give these frame rates.

  • 16.7 ms per frame is 60 FPS.
  • 25 ms per frame is 40 FPS.
  • 50 ms per frame is 20 FPS.

So a 1% low of 40 means that one frame in every hundred took 25 ms or longer. In a game running at an average of 100 FPS, that is about one slow frame every second.

Tools work it out in slightly different ways. Some take the frame time at the 99th percentile, the point that 99% of frames are faster than. Others average all the frames in the slowest 1%. The second way gives a lower number. Compare 1% lows only between runs measured with the same tool. The 0.1% low is the same idea for the slowest one frame in a thousand. It catches rare big hitches.

Why the 1% low matters more than the average for smoothness

Your eyes notice changes in pace more than the pace itself. A steady 60 FPS feels smooth. A game that runs at 140 FPS but drops a few frames to 30 every second feels worse, even though its average is more than double. Each slow frame is a tiny freeze, and you feel it as a hitch when you turn the camera.

That is why reviewers show the 1% low next to the average. Two graphics cards with the same average can feel different, and the 1% low is where that shows.

What is a good 1% low?

There isn't an official number. What matters is the gap to your average. This is RigSight's own rule of thumb, not an industry standard.

  • A 1% low at 70% of the average or higher plays smoothly. 100 average with 75 lows is fine.
  • Between 50 and 70% of the average, you may feel the odd stutter.
  • Below half of the average, the game stutters. 100 average with 40 lows feels rough.

Two more checks help. If you have a G-Sync or FreeSync monitor, try to keep the 1% low above the bottom of its variable refresh range, which is in the monitor's specs. And for fast shooters, a higher 1% low is usually worth more than a higher average.

How to see your 1% low

These are the common ways to see it.

  • MSI Afterburner with RivaTuner Statistics Server. In Afterburner's settings, open Monitoring, tick Framerate 1% Low and turn on Show in On-Screen Display. RivaTuner must be running to draw the overlay. MSI notes the 1% low may only update while benchmark recording runs, so set a benchmark hotkey on the Benchmark tab and press it in game.
  • CapFrameX, a free tool that records a run and shows the averages, the lows and a frame time graph.
  • Many games' own benchmark modes, which print average and lows at the end.

Measure the same stretch of play each time, and play it for a minute or more. A 1% low from ten seconds of play swings a lot from run to run.

How to fix 1% low FPS drops

When the 1% low is far below the average, look for something that interrupts the game now and then. Try these in order.

  1. Close background work. Launchers downloading updates, browsers playing video and cloud sync all grab CPU and disk time at random moments. Press Ctrl + Shift + Esc and sort Processes by CPU and Disk.
  2. Check graphics memory. If video memory sits at your card's full size, the game swaps data in and out, and you get spikes. Lower Texture quality first.
  3. Check for a CPU limit. Poor lows with low GPU load in busy scenes usually mean the CPU is the limit. GPU not at 100% usage but low FPS shows the test.
  4. Set a frame cap. A cap a little below what your PC holds in busy scenes trades a few frames of average for a much steadier pace. With G-Sync or FreeSync, cap a few frames below the refresh rate. VSync vs an FPS cap explains why.
  5. Give it a second session after updates. After a new graphics driver or game patch, many games rebuild their shaders while you play, and the first session hitches. If it stays bad, see FPS drops in games.
  6. Rule out heat. A GPU or CPU at its limit slows down in bursts. Normal GPU temperature while gaming has the numbers.
  7. Set Windows to Best performance in Settings > System > Power & battery > Power mode, and play laptops on the charger.
