Your PC fans are loud while gaming, or they got louder than they used to be and you want to know why. Some of that is normal. A game makes the GPU and often the CPU draw far more power than the desktop does, and the fans speed up to move the heat out. But a PC that got noisier for the same games usually has a cause you can find, and most of them are easy to fix.

Find which fan is loud

"The fans" can be any of four or five things, and each has a different fix. Start by working out which one it is.

  • GPU fans. They follow the graphics card's temperature. Loud in games, quiet or stopped on the desktop.
  • CPU cooler fan, or the radiator fans on a liquid cooler. They follow the CPU temperature and often rev up and down quickly.
  • Case fans. Often set to follow the CPU temperature too, unless you changed it.
  • Power supply fan. Some stay off at light load and spin up when the PC draws a lot of power.
  • The pump on a liquid cooler, which can hum or gurgle rather than whoosh.

Listen with the side panel off while a game runs, keeping fingers and cables away from the blades. Then open a monitoring app that lists every fan with its speed and watch which one climbs when the noise starts. How to check fan speed on PC covers the tools.

Check the temperatures

Loud fans are only a problem if they're loud for a reason that's wrong. Look at the GPU and CPU temperatures in the same game. If they're in the normal range, the fans are doing their job and the fix is about noise, not heat. If they're high even with the fans flat out, the cooling isn't keeping up, and PC overheating while gaming is the better place to start. For the GPU side, see normal GPU temperature while gaming.

Cap your frame rate

This is the quickest fix for GPU fan noise. With no limit, the GPU renders as many frames as it can, even in a menu or an old game where it could make 500 a second. It works flat out, makes all the heat it can, and the fans follow.

  1. In the game's settings, turn on V-Sync or set a frame rate limit at or just under your monitor's refresh rate.
  2. Or set it for every game. In the NVIDIA Control Panel, open Manage 3D settings and set Max Frame Rate. In AMD Software, Radeon Chill can hold the frame rate under a limit you set.

A 144 Hz screen can't show more than 144 frames a second, so a GPU making 300 is working hard for frames you never see. Capping it often drops the fan speed a lot without changing what you see.

Clean out the dust

Dust on the fan blades and in the fins of a heatsink stops air getting through. The parts run hotter, so the fans have to spin faster to do the same job. It builds up slowly, which is why a PC can get louder over months without anything changing.

  1. Shut down, switch the power supply off and unplug the PC.
  2. Take the side panel off and clean the dust filters, usually at the front, top and bottom.
  3. Blow dust out of the CPU heatsink, the GPU and the radiator with compressed air made for electronics, in short bursts.
  4. Hold each fan still with a finger while you blow on it, so the air doesn't spin it faster than it's made to turn.

Keep a vacuum cleaner out of the case. Dell's own cleaning guide says vacuums create static, which can damage the parts.

Smooth out the fan curves

A fan curve says how fast a fan spins at each temperature. Many default curves are steep, so a short CPU spike, like a game loading a level, makes the fans jump up and back down again. That rise and fall is often more annoying than a steady, louder hum.

Motherboard fans are set in the BIOS or UEFI. Restart and press Delete or F2 as the PC starts, then look for a fan page. The name varies by maker. ASUS calls it Q-Fan Control, Gigabyte and MSI call it Smart Fan, and ASRock calls it FAN-Tastic Tuning. Most of these pages let you do the following.

  • Pick a quieter preset, such as Silent or Standard.
  • Flatten the curve so the fans don't climb until the CPU passes about 60 to 70°C.
  • Add a delay or step time, so the fans react slowly to short spikes.

Keep the top of the curve at full speed, so the fans can still save the CPU if it gets hot. You set GPU fan curves in the card's software. That can be MSI Afterburner, the card maker's tool, or Performance > Tuning in AMD Software. The defaults there are usually sensible, so only change them if you know what each step does.

Why are my PC fans suddenly loud?

If the noise started on a certain day, something changed on that day. These are the things to check.

  • Background work. Open Task Manager with Ctrl+Shift+Esc, go to Processes and sort by CPU and then by GPU. A Windows update, an antivirus scan, a game launcher installing an update or a browser tab with a video can all keep the PC busy. That's also the usual reason for PC fans loud when not gaming.
  • Shaders being built. After a game or driver update, many games build their shaders again in the first minutes. The CPU works hard and the fans ramp up, then it settles.
  • A BIOS update or reset. Updating the BIOS, or clearing its settings, puts the fans back on the default curve. If you had a quiet curve before, set it again.
  • A new driver or fan tool. A graphics driver update, or a tool like MSI Afterburner loading a profile, can change the GPU fan curve.
  • A new game or setting. A heavier game, a higher resolution or a new upscaler setting can push the GPU harder than before.
  • The room. A warm summer evening means warmer air going in, so the fans spin faster for the same temperature.

A sound that changed, like grinding, clicking, rattling or a high whine, is different. That's usually a worn bearing or something touching the blades. Find the fan with the steps above, check no cable is in its way, and replace it if the sound stays. Fans are cheap, and a worn one tends to get louder.

How RigSight makes this easier

RigSight is a free, open source app that records your PC all day, so you can look back at when the noise started instead of trying to catch it live. The Fans page lists every fan your PC reports and says what each one follows, such as "Speeds up with your GPU", "Speeds up with your CPU" or "Runs at a steady speed". That tells you which fan is which without opening the case.

Pick a day, a week, a month or a year, and the page names the game or app that made the fans work hardest, the fastest speed with what you were doing at the time, and the loudest hour, day or month. Each fan has a list of what made it spin, so you can see if a game or a background app is the one pushing it.

If a motherboard fan comes back from a restart at another speed, as after a change in the BIOS, the Timeline shows it as "Fan speeds changed while the PC was off", which helps when the noise started after a restart. And if a fan spins slower than usual at the same temperature, the page marks it as needing a look, since that can be dust or a worn fan. Motherboard fans need RigSight to run with admin rights. GPU fans show either way.

Start with the frame rate cap and Task Manager tonight. If the noise is the same, look at the temperatures and dust next, and only then reach for the fan curves.
