You opened Event Viewer after a random restart and found a red Critical entry: Kernel-Power, event 41, "The system has rebooted without cleanly shutting down first." Kernel Power 41 sounds like a diagnosis. It isn't. It's Windows saying it found itself switched back on without a proper shutdown, and the clues to why are inside the event.

What Kernel-Power 41 actually means

When you shut down normally, Windows closes everything and marks the shutdown as clean. When it starts and finds no clean mark, it logs event 41 from the source Kernel-Power. So the event is written at the next start, not at the moment the PC went down, and its time is the time it started again.

Anything that stops Windows without a proper shutdown leaves one behind. A blue screen, a power cut, holding the power button, a hard freeze, a power supply that gives out, a PC that shuts itself off from heat: all of them end in the same event. That's why searching the event ID alone gets you every possible answer.

Read the fields on the Details tab

  1. Right-click Start and open Event Viewer.
  2. Go to Windows Logs > System, click Filter Current Log and type 41 in the event IDs box.
  3. Double-click the event, then open the Details tab and pick Friendly View.

These are the fields that matter:

  • BugcheckCode: the blue screen's stop code, written in decimal. 0 means no blue screen was recorded. Any other number means Windows crashed with a stop error. Convert it to hex to look it up: open Calculator, switch to Programmer, type the number with DEC selected and read the HEX line. Microsoft's own example, 159, is 0x9F, DRIVER_POWER_STATE_FAILURE.
  • PowerButtonTimestamp: anything other than 0 means someone held the power button to switch the PC off.
  • SleepInProgress: the name suggests yes or no, and Microsoft's event 41 article only shows it as false or 0. On many PCs it holds another number. Microsoft doesn't explain those numbers for this event, but they line up with Windows' list of power states: 0 is none, 2 to 4 are sleep, 5 is hibernate and 6 is off. Read that way, a 5 or 6 means Windows was going into hibernate or switching off when it stopped. With Fast Startup on, a normal shutdown goes through hibernate. So a 5 or 6 most likely means a shutdown that never finished, not a crash in the middle of a game.

Microsoft's own advice splits it the same way. A code above 0 is a stop error, so follow the stop code: see our blue screen guide. A power button timestamp means the PC was switched off by hand, usually because it had frozen. When everything is 0, Windows had no chance to write anything, and Microsoft points to the power supply, overheating, memory and overclocking.

What causes Kernel Power 41 with a code of 0

  • A power cut or dip. If the lights flickered or other devices lost power too, it was the mains. A battery backup, called a UPS, stops these.
  • A power supply that can't keep up. This is the classic cause of Kernel Power 41 while gaming. Modern graphics cards can draw short spikes above their rated power, and a tired or undersized supply switches off to protect itself. It looks exactly like a power cut from inside the PC.
  • Overheating. CPUs and GPUs switch the PC off when they pass their limit, rather than burn out. The limit is on the maker's page for your model and differs between models. Intel lists 100°C for the Core i9-14900K, for example, AMD 95°C for most Ryzen 7000 chips and 89°C for the X3D ones.
  • An unstable overclock or undervolt. That includes XMP or EXPO memory profiles, Curve Optimizer and GPU tuning tools. Tuning that is too tight can reset the PC with no blue screen.
  • A held power button after a freeze. The timestamp usually records it, but Microsoft notes it can stay at 0 when the frozen PC couldn't write to its drive.
  • A shutdown that didn't finish, often with Fast Startup. Check SleepInProgress: a 5 or 6 here is usually harmless.
  • A loose cable. A power cable at the wall, at the supply, or the GPU's own power plugs.

How to fix it, in order

  1. Note when it happens. Only under load points to the power supply or heat. At idle or overnight points to the mains, sleep or Fast Startup.
  2. Undo all tuning. In the BIOS, load the default settings and turn off XMP or EXPO for a test. Reset any GPU overclock in its tool. If the restarts stop, the tuning was the cause.
  3. Check temperatures while gaming. If the CPU or GPU is near its limit just before it goes off, clean the dust filters and check every fan spins.
  4. Plug the PC straight into a wall socket, not a power strip or extension. Reseat the power supply's cables at both ends, and the GPU's power plugs.
  5. Check the power supply. Compare its wattage with your graphics card maker's recommended supply. If the card takes two or three 8-pin plugs, check the manuals for the card and the power supply. Some ask for a separate cable from the supply for each plug instead of one cable with two plugs on it, and with a power-hungry card that is the safer choice anyway.
  6. If the 41s happen at shutdown, sleep or wake, turn off Fast Startup: Control Panel > Hardware and Sound > Power Options > Choose what the power buttons do > Change settings that are currently unavailable, then clear Turn on fast startup and click Save changes. Update the BIOS and chipset drivers from your motherboard maker too.
  7. If it still happens under load with everything at defaults, test with another power supply. It's the part most often at fault, and the hardest to prove any other way.

Also check whether the 41 is alone. Filter the System log for WHEA-Logger as the event source. An error the hardware couldn't correct around the same time points at the CPU, memory or motherboard, or at tuning that's too tight for them.

Where RigSight helps with Kernel-Power 41

The hard part of Kernel-Power 41 is that all zeros tell you almost nothing. The answer is in what the PC was doing just before, and Windows doesn't keep that. RigSight, a free and open-source Windows monitor, records it every minute and reads event 41 for you.

On its Crashes page, every sudden shut-off reads the event's fields and gets a cause with one word for how sure it is. A held power button reads as Confirmed. A SleepInProgress of 5 or 6 says Windows was already shutting down. An error the hardware couldn't correct, logged around it, makes it Likely a hardware fault. RigSight is careful with the word power: a shut-off is only "Likely a power cut" when the PC was doing little, nobody held the power button and Windows logged no error. A shut-off under load is only "Possibly mains or power supply", because from inside the PC the two look the same, and it says so.

Open a shut-off and you see the 45 minutes before it: temperatures, load, GPU power draw, memory and fans, with the game in front. If the GPU was drawing 320 W at normal temperatures when it went off, that points toward the power supply rather than heat. The shut-off is timed by the last minute RigSight recorded, not by Windows' 6008 time, which can be some minutes early. CPU temperature and power need RigSight running with admin rights.
